The "Volunteer of the Month" award for outstanding contributors.

How to Apply
Interested students should download the application form from www.greencity.org and send it to volunteer@greencity.org before June 10th. For further information, call us at 555-0199.

When I was twelve years old, my father gave me an old, dusty camera. While other kids were busy playing football, I spent my afternoons wandering through the narrow streets of our town, trying to catch the way sunlight hit the brick walls. However, my enthusiasm was not shared by everyone. My art teacher, Mr. Jenkins, once looked at my photos and remarked, "Photography is just pressing a button. True art requires a brush and a soul." His words felt like a heavy stone in my heart. For a few weeks, I put the camera away, feeling that my passion was somehow "lesser" than traditional painting.

One rainy afternoon, I saw an elderly man sitting alone at a bus stop, holding a worn-out umbrella. There was a look of such deep peace on his face that I instinctively reached for my camera and snapped a picture. When I developed the film, the image moved me in a way I hadn't expected. It wasn't just a man; it was a story of time, patience, and quiet strength.

I decided to show the photo to Mr. Jenkins. He stared at it for a long time without saying a word. Finally, he looked up and said, "I was wrong. You didn't just press a button; you noticed something that others would have walked past. That is the soul of an artist."

That experience taught me that we should not let the opinions of others define our worth. Everyone has their own way of seeing the world, and as long as we stay true to our vision, our work will eventually find its voice.

For many years, the concept of "multitasking" was praised as a necessary skill for success in the modern world. We believed that by doing several things at once—answering emails while attending a meeting, or studying while listening to a podcast—我们正在提高效率。然而，最近心理学研究表明，所谓的多任务处理其实是一个误区。

Actually, the human brain is not designed to focus on two complex tasks simultaneously. Instead, it "switches" its attention back and forth between tasks. Each time we switch, there is a "switching cost"—a brief delay where the brain must refocus. While these delays only last a fraction of a second, they add up. Studies show that multitasking can reduce productivity by as much as 40%.

Beyond efficiency, multitasking also affects the quality of our work. When our attention is divided, we are more likely to make errors and less likely to remember what we have learned. This is particularly concerning in educational settings. Students who multitask during lectures tend to have lower test scores than those who stay focused on the teacher.

Furthermore, constant task-switching can lead to increased stress and mental fatigue. The brain feels exhausted because it is constantly being forced to restart. In the long run, this habit may even weaken our ability to engage in "deep work"—the capacity to focus without distraction on a cognitively demanding task.

In a society that values speed, it takes courage to slow down. By practicing "single-tasking"—doing one thing at a time with full intention—we can not only improve our performance but also rediscover a sense of calm in our busy lives.
