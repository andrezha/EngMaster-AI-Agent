For a long time, the concept of "speed reading" was promoted as a crucial technique for success in the fast-paced modern era. We assumed that by scanning multiple pages at once—skimming through reports while taking a phone call, or flipping through a textbook while watching a video—we were boosting our comprehension. However, recent cognitive research indicates that so-called speed reading is actually a misconception.

In reality, the human brain is not built to process two dense sources of information at the same time. Instead, it "alternates" its focus back and forth between different inputs. Every time we alternate, there is a "cognitive penalty"—a temporary lag where the brain has to adjust. Although these lags only take a fraction of a second, they accumulate. Studies demonstrate that dividing attention can reduce retention by as much as 40%.

Beyond comprehension, rapid switching also affects the depth of our understanding. When our focus is split, we are more likely to overlook details and less likely to retain the material. This is especially problematic in academic environments. Students who divide their attention between reading and listening to background music tend to achieve lower scores than those who focus entirely on the text.

Moreover, constant information-switching can result in elevated anxiety and mental exhaustion. The mind feels drained because it is constantly forced to reset its focus. Over time, this practice might even weaken our ability to engage in "deep reflection"—the capacity to contemplate without distraction on a complex problem.

In a culture that prioritizes haste, it requires patience to slow down. By adopting "slow reading"—reading one passage at a time with full concentration—we can not only enhance our comprehension but also regain a feeling of peace in our demanding lives.
