TITLE: Cultural Nuances in Communication
A few years ago, I was invited to a dinner by a colleague from a different country. I received an email from the host that said, “I would be quite happy if you could attend.” That “quite” made me worry. I thought he was saying, “I don’t mind if you come, but I’m not very excited about it.” Later, I discovered that in his culture, “quite” often means “very,” while in my own, it means “somewhat.”
The first lesson about social life in other cultures is to learn the language, and I don’t just mean the vocabulary. It is the tone, gestures, manners, and beliefs. The way people interact emphasizes many of the differences we see between cultures.
Some of these differences may be only on the surface—clothing, food, or greeting styles—while others may be deeper and take longer to understand. Mostly, it is just a matter of getting used to the differences and respecting them, just as one respects the local weather while traveling for business.
Some differences can be seen as an improvement. People might be more helpful; the hospitality is warmer; you ask for directions and they walk you to your destination. However, other differences can be confusing, like the timing of gifts. If you are invited to a house at 6 o’clock, your hosts might expect a small gift immediately in Switzerland, after the meal in parts of Asia, the next day in some Mediterranean areas, or not at all in other regions. I prefer not to use the word “wrong” because there is nothing incorrect about these customs. It is simply the accepted way of doing things in their own society.
