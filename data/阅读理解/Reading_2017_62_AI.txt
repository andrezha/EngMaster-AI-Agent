TITLE: Saving Traditional Puppetry
Some of the world’s top artists recently met in Beijing and Xi’an to celebrate the first International Shadow Puppetry Day. Recently, a global cultural group set June 20 as a day to increase awareness of this art, its value, and its potential as a shared language across different cultures.
Despite the event, the audience for shadow puppetry in many regions continues to shrink and get older. The art has struggled to attract younger generations. It is Li Hua’s job to change that. As an artistic adviser for the National Arts Center, Li hopes to grow the audience, make the art easier to access, and protect its history.
“Shadow puppetry seems like it is no longer a part of the modern taste,” Li tells a reporter. “What I hope to achieve is that my generation and younger people start to look at it again. It is not just old-fashioned anymore. It is actually colorful, and it can be digital.”
Li says one problem today is that the fun side of the art has been lost. “The art cannot be shown today the way it was 100 years ago. It must continue to change, because the way the world lives is not the same,” says Li.
Last year, Li worked on a project that combined traditional puppet stories with modern electronic music for a youth festival. “I wanted to remind people that this art is for entertainment as much as it is for history,” says Li. “For me, it is about putting it in a new context. In art, where does the feeling lie? Can we learn more about our own emotions through these stories? Sometimes we forget that art has a wider meaning,” says Li, “so I want to keep those conversations going. Those are the things I want to encourage.”
