TITLE: Lessons from the Flight Deck
It is often said that experienced specialists make poor team players because they think they already have all the answers. I was fortunate; I became a licensed pilot in 1975, years before I started my career as a senior engineer. I didn't realize it then, but learning to fly made me a better project manager. I loved the challenge. As I operated more complex machines in difficult conditions, I learned about Collaborative Team Management (CTM), a concept designed to make flying safer. It suggests that every team member should listen and speak up to achieve the best result, regardless of their rank.
I first encountered CTM in the early 1980s. Soon after, a senior supervisor and I were flying in heavy fog. The ground control gave us a turn instruction too late for a smooth landing. The supervisor was at the controls, while I was the safety observer. He was so focused on the difficult turn that he forgot to lower the landing gear. He was much more experienced than I was --- and he was my boss --- so it felt uncomfortable to correct him. But I had to: our safety was at risk. I pushed past my hesitation and shouted, "We need to lower the gear now!" That was my first true lesson in the importance of CTM, and I have applied it in the engineering lab ever since.
CTM requires the leader to encourage others to share their views. It also requires that when someone offers a different opinion, the leader does not react poorly, which might stop others from speaking up in the future. Now, when I am leading a project, I always ask for input and help from the team. Sometimes they are hesitant to talk. But I hope that if I keep encouraging them, one day someone will stop me from making a critical "gear up" mistake.
