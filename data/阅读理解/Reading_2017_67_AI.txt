TITLE: The Silent Language of Plants
When a plant is being harmed, it does not just suffer in silence. In the 1980s, researchers Jack Schultz and Ian Baldwin found that young trees attacked by pests release a specific scent that nearby plants can detect. These chemicals come from the damaged parts of the plant and act as a warning signal. The substances plants release into the air are a group of chemicals called volatile organic compounds, or VOCs.
Scientists have discovered that nearly all plants release VOCs when they are under threat. It is a plant’s version of a distress call. But is anyone paying attention? It seems so, because we can observe how neighboring plants respond.
Some plants release strong-smelling chemicals to drive pests away. Others have a different strategy. They release scents designed to attract other insects that are natural enemies of the pests. Once these helpers arrive, the situation changes completely. The pest that was eating the plant is now the one being eaten.
In many studies, it seems that these chemical signals help the neighbors. The damage is usually worse on the first plant, but the surrounding plants stay safer because they received the warning and prepared themselves. Does this mean plants truly talk to each other? Scientists are not sure. Perhaps the first plant was simply reacting to pain or sending a signal to its own other branches. In that case, it was talking to itself. Maybe the neighbors just "overheard" the signal. So information was shared, but it might not have been a purposeful conversation.
Long ago, Charles Darwin imagined a world much more active and connected than the one we see. Our senses are limited, but there is a great deal happening that we cannot perceive.
