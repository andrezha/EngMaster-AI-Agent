TITLE: The Vanishing Monarch Butterflies
Monarch butterflies once filled the skies across North America in massive numbers. Early records from the 1800s described orange-and-black clouds so thick that they could be seen from miles away as they migrated.
Scientists estimated that during their peak, there were billions of Monarchs --- making up a significant portion of all butterfly species in the region. They were considered perhaps the most common butterflies on Earth. Even as recently as the 1990s, when their population had already begun to fall, millions of butterflies were still found clustered in the forests of Mexico during the winter.
Unfortunately, their large numbers may have led to their downfall. Because they seemed so common, people assumed the population would always be there and paid little attention to the destruction of their habitat. Land developers cleared fields of milkweed --- the only plant Monarch larvae eat --- to build homes and farms. Large-scale farming also used chemicals that killed these essential plants.
By the early 2000s, the natural areas where Monarchs bred had been heavily damaged by the human need for more land and industrial growth. This forced the butterflies into smaller, isolated areas, where extreme weather and cold winters further reduced their numbers. Soon, the great migrations began to fade.
In recent years, several states have passed laws to protect Monarch habitats, but by then, the population had already dropped by nearly 90 percent. While some butterflies still survive through the help of conservation groups and "butterfly gardens" in people’s backyards, the sight of a sky filled with millions of Monarchs is becoming a memory of the past.
