TITLE: The Blocks Test
Four years ago, while teaching design at a college in Chicago, I used a set of wooden blocks as an initial test to understand my students' personalities. I placed a box of blocks in front of each person and said: “Create something unique. You have one hour today—and one hour each day for the rest of the week.”
A few students were slow to begin. They watched their neighbors to see what the popular choice would be. Several others carefully read the provided booklet and built a structure exactly like the picture on the cover. Another group began building something entirely from their own thoughts.
Once, I had a student who spent all his spare time experimenting with these blocks. His complex structures filled the shelves in the lab and a large part of his apartment. I was thrilled to have such a student. He possessed a remarkably inventive mind. His presence meant I had a natural leader in the room whose imagination would inspire others.
Promoting this type of open-ended thinking has a drawback. I risked discouraging those students who preferred clear instructions and structure. Frequently, one would say, “But I just don’t have an inventive mind.”
“Do you ever imagine stories when you are daydreaming?”
“Well, yes.”
“Then tell me one of your most vivid daydreams.” The student would describe something incredibly detailed—exploring a distant planet or living in a city under the sea. “That is very imaginative. Who created that for you?”
“No one. I did.”
“So you did it while you were sitting here?”
“Yes.”
“Then try doing the same thing with these blocks, okay?”
