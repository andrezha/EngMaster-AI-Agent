TITLE: The Secret of Lasting Memory
Adults are often amazed by how well they can recall skills they learned as children but have not used for a long time. A person who hasn't been in a pool for twenty years can still swim easily once they get back into the water. Someone can pick up a guitar after a decade and still play a few songs. A father can easily show his son how to throw a baseball or remember the exact rules of a childhood game. A teacher who hasn't looked at a specific textbook for years can still recite a classic poem or tell a famous historical story from memory.
One reason for this is the principle of overlearning. This rule suggests that once we have mastered a skill or a piece of information, continuing to practice it even more will significantly increase how long we remember it.
During childhood, we usually keep practicing basic skills like riding a bike or playing sports long after we have first learned the basics. We repeat nursery rhymes and listen to the same stories over and over. We don't just learn them; we overlearn them.
Common knowledge, such as basic math facts or the alphabet, is an exception to the rule that we quickly forget what we studied in school. This is because these are things we overlearned during our early years of education.
The principle of overlearning explains why "last-minute cramming" for a test is not an effective way to master a subject, even if it helps a student pass. By cramming, a student might memorize enough to get a good grade, but they will likely forget almost everything within a few weeks. On the other hand, a bit of overlearning is vital for one’s long-term personal and professional growth.
