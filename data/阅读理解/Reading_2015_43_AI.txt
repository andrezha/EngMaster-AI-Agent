TITLE: The Dialogue Lab: Solving Urban Loneliness
Deep conversation is the main focus tonight at the Dialogue Lab. This evening, as they do every Friday, social worker Sarah Miller is leading one of the city’s favorite new activities: drinking tea and engaging in "open sharing." Here, participants are learning to express their inner thoughts. It is not always easy. The guests — around twenty professionals who pay a small fee of $5 per session — are often quick to talk about work projects but slow to share personal feelings. "You are not allowed to say 'some people feel' or 'it is believed'," Miller tells them. "Say 'I feel,' or 'To me'."
A community center where work talk is banned? It seems unusual for a busy business district. But Miller’s project is about more than personal growth; it is an attempt to help the city's struggling traditional teahouses. Over the years, urban teahouses have suffered from changes in modern lifestyles — longer commute times, the rise of home delivery apps, and the tendency of the younger generation to stay online. Dozens of new "theme labs" have appeared to change this. Places focused on philosophy, storytelling, and coding are becoming popular, keeping tables busy until late at night.
The city’s "sharing labs," which provide emotional support, are among the most visited spots. Young professionals, recent graduates, and those looking for work come to these places to talk about stress, hope, and career paths with a guide. They join Miller’s group simply to learn how to say what they truly mean. "There is a massive need for real connection in this city," says David Green, a regular at the Dialogue Lab who works as a software engineer nearby. "People have many online contacts but few real friends. They need to be heard." Miller says she hopes to see these labs across the country. "If people had balanced lives, these places wouldn't be needed," she says. "But as long as life is a race, people need a special space to be themselves."
