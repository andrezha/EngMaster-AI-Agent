TITLE: The Value of Being Likable
During my early school years, I was happy to share my snacks and jokes, which helped me stay popular. I was the leader of the playground. Then came my teenage years, with its "cool" groups. These students gained status not by being kind, but by ignoring rules and being sarcastic toward others. I soon found myself trying to follow them.
Popularity is a major topic in social psychology. Dr. Robert Miller, a psychologist, divides the popular into two types: the "likable" and the "status-seekers." The likable individuals have qualities that help them build strong friendships and social skills, which they use later in their careers. Then there is the teenage version of popularity: status built on power and sometimes negative behavior.
While the "cool" teens might seem successful, Dr. Miller’s research shows some negative results. Those who sought high status in high school are more likely to engage in risky or unhealthy behaviors as adults.
In one study, Dr. Miller tracked 200 teenagers, comparing the most liked students with the highest-status ones. "We found that the high-status teens became more aggressive toward their peers over time. Likability leads to healthy social growth, while high status often has the opposite effect," he noted.
Dr. Miller also found that the traits that make a person a good friend—kindness, honesty, and openness—help them connect with others much better in adult life.
In his analysis, Dr. Miller reached another conclusion: Likability does more than just lead to a good life; it actively helps create it. "Being liked opens doors for new experiences and learning that give a person a clear advantage," he said.
