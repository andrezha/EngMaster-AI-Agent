TITLE: The Reluctant Mentor
“You can contact me as a last option, and if no one else is available, then I will help.” This was the response from a senior developer after I asked for mentors for our new training program.
I assumed he had a busy project schedule or perhaps some hesitation about teaching others. He might just need a little encouragement. So I tried again and shared some stories. I mentioned the junior developer who was struggling alone and talked about the manager who was leading a team in an area he didn't even specialize in... At this point, the senior developer said, “Fine. I’ll do it.”
I was relieved because I knew the benefit of spreading mentorship among many people. The reluctant mentor began reviewing code, sending helpful links, and giving career advice. Eventually, he became a key member of the program. The other leads could focus on their specific tasks while the juniors felt supported. Helping a new coder solve a difficult problem can be as satisfying as finishing your own project.
Still, most of us mentors feel a sense of relief when the training period ends. But that feeling is mixed with a realization of why the same people keep helping: Connecting with the professional community as you give your time and skills provides real satisfaction. Helping others just feels good.
In that way, I think mentoring is more of a personal reward than I’d like to admit. But if others grow in the process and I feel good too, does the original reason really matter?
