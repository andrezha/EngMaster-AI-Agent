When I was thirteen years old, my uncle gifted me an old, well-worn set of sketching charcoal. While other classmates spent their weekends playing tennis, I sat on my bedroom floor, trying to shade the way light curved along the edges. To me, drawing was a quiet language that allowed me to convey emotions I could not express in daily conversations.

However, my enthusiasm was not appreciated by everyone. My physics teacher, Mr. Hill, saw my creations once and remarked, "Art is just smudging black powder on a surface. True intelligence requires solving calculations and reading textbooks." His words felt like a heavy weight on my spirit. For a month, I put my charcoal away, feeling that my hobby was somehow inferior to academic study.

One cold afternoon, I saw a stray dog sheltering under a low metal bench. There was an expression of such quiet resilience in its eyes that I instinctively reached for my pad and began to sketch. When I completed the portrait, it affected me in a way I had not anticipated. It wasn't just a simple animal sketch; it was a story of endurance, patience, and silent beauty.

I decided to show the sketch to Mr. Hill. He observed it for a long time without saying a word. Finally, he looked up and said, "I was mistaken. You didn't just smudge blocks of material; you noticed details that others would have ignored. That is the true gift of an observer."

That experience taught me that we should not let the opinions of others define our worth. Everyone sees the world differently, and as long as we stay faithful to our vision, our efforts will eventually find their own voice.

For many years, the idea of "speed-calculation" was promoted as a necessary skill for success in the modern business world. We believed that by processing several financial reports at once—answering messages while checking figures—we were increasing our daily output. However, recent productivity research suggests that so-called speed-calculation is actually a major disadvantage.

In reality, the human mind is not designed to focus on two different sets of numbers at once. Instead, it "alternates" its attention between tasks. Each time we shift focus, there is an "interruption cost"—a brief delay where the brain must adjust to the new subject. While these delays last only a few seconds, they accumulate. Studies show that divided focus can lower calculation quality by as much as 35%.

Beyond output, divided attention also affects the clarity of our data. When our concentration is split, we are more likely to include numerical errors and less likely to convey our findings clearly. This is especially problematic in academic or legal reports. Analysts who multitask during data processing tend to have lower approval ratings for their performance.

Furthermore, constant task-switching can cause increased stress and mental fatigue. The mind feels worn out because it is repeatedly forced to start and stop. In the long run, this habit may even weaken our ability to engage in "deep thinking"—the capacity to focus without distraction on complex numerical tasks.

In a society that praises quick results, it takes discipline to slow down. By practicing "focused processing"—calculating one report at a time with full attention—we can not only enhance our analysis but also regain a sense of tranquility in our demanding daily routines.
