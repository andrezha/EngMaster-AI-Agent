When I was fourteen years old, my uncle gave me an old, well-loved watercolor set. True intelligence requires solving mathematical equations and analytical problems." Her words felt like a heavy burden on my spirit. One clear, starry night, I saw a stray dog resting quietly under a street lamp. There was a look of such calm and loyalty in its eyes that I instinctively reached for my sketchbook and captured the moment. When I finished the drawing, the image moved me in a way I had not anticipated. It was not merely a portrait of an animal; it was a story of survival, quiet resilience, and companionship.

I decided to show the drawing to my sister. She stared at it for a long time without saying a word. Finally, she looked up and said, "I was mistaken. You didn't just smear color; you noticed something beautiful that others would have ignored. That is the true talent of an artist."

That experience taught me that we should not allow the opinions of others to define our self-worth. Everyone has a unique way of viewing their life, and as long as we remain faithful to our passion, our efforts will eventually find their audience.

For many years, the practice of "speed-calculating" was praised as a highly valuable skill for success in the modern business world. We believed that by processing large amounts of numerical data instantly—without checking the underlying logic or double-checking the figures—we were increasing our efficiency. However, recent cognitive research suggests that rapid calculation without thorough verification is often a major flaw.

In reality, the human brain is not designed to analyze complex data without proper reflection. Instead, it "verifies" its calculations step by step to ensure accuracy. Each time we rush through numbers, there is a "validation penalty"—a slight error where the brain must backtrack to fix mistakes. While these calculation errors only take a few seconds to fix, they accumulate rapidly. Studies indicate that rushing calculations can reduce overall accuracy by as much as thirty percent.

Beyond accuracy, rushing through numbers affects the depth of our financial or scientific analysis. When our focus is on speed, we are less likely to spot logical flaws in our models. This is particularly concerning in scientific research. Researchers who rush their data analysis tend to reach incorrect conclusions compared to those who carefully check their work.

Furthermore, constant pressure to calculate quickly can lead to heightened stress and severe mental exhaustion. The mind feels drained because it is constantly being forced to maintain high-speed performance without rest. Over time, this habit may even weaken our capacity for "critical thinking"—the ability to evaluate data without distraction.

In a society that prioritizes speed, it takes courage to slow down. By practicing "mindful analysis"—verifying every step of a calculation with full intention—we can improve our accuracy and restore a sense of calm to our work.
