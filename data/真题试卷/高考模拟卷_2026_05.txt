When I was thirteen years old, my grandfather gifted me an old, well-worn set of woodworking tools. While other classmates spent their weekends playing soccer, I sat on my bedroom floor, trying to carve the way wood grain curved along the edges. To me, woodworking was a quiet language that allowed me to convey emotions I could not express in daily conversations.

However, my enthusiasm was not appreciated by everyone. My history teacher, Mr. Davis, saw my creations once and remarked, "Craftsmanship is just shaping blocks of wood. True intelligence requires reading historical documents and writing essays." His words felt like a heavy weight on my spirit. For a month, I put my tools away, feeling that my hobby was somehow inferior to academic study.

One cold afternoon, I saw a stray cat sheltering under a low wooden bench. There was an expression of such quiet resilience in its eyes that I instinctively reached for my materials and began to carve. When I completed the wooden figure, it affected me in a way I had not anticipated. It wasn't just a simple animal carving; it was a story of endurance, patience, and silent beauty.

I decided to show the carving to Mr. Davis. He observed it for a long time without saying a word. Finally, he looked up and said, "I was mistaken. You didn't just shape blocks of material; you noticed details that others would have ignored. That is the true gift of an observer."

That experience taught me that we should not let the opinions of others define our worth. Everyone sees the world differently, and as long as we stay faithful to our vision, our efforts will eventually find their own voice.

For many years, the idea of "speed-speaking" was promoted as a necessary skill for success in the modern business world. We believed that by delivering multiple presentations at once—addressing different clients while summarizing reports—we were increasing our daily output. However, recent productivity research suggests that so-called speed-speaking is actually a major disadvantage.

In reality, the human mind is not designed to focus on two different speaking topics at once. Instead, it "alternates" its attention between tasks. Each time we shift focus, there is an "interruption cost"—a brief delay where the brain must adjust to the new subject. While these delays last only a few seconds, they accumulate. Studies show that divided focus can lower presentation quality by as much as 35%.

Beyond output, divided attention also affects the clarity of our communication. When our concentration is split, we are more likely to include grammatical errors and less likely to convey our ideas clearly. This is especially problematic in academic or legal speeches. Speakers who multitask during presentations tend to have lower approval ratings for their performance.

Furthermore, constant task-switching can cause increased stress and mental fatigue. The mind feels worn out because it is repeatedly forced to start and stop. In the long run, this habit may even weaken our ability to engage in "deep thinking"—the capacity to focus without distraction on complex speaking tasks.

In a society that praises quick results, it takes discipline to slow down. By practicing "focused speaking"—presenting one topic at a time with full attention—we can not only enhance our communication but also regain a sense of tranquility in our demanding daily routines.
