For many years, the concept of "rapid communication" was praised as a necessary skill for success in the modern world. We believed that by handling several channels simultaneously—responding to text messages while attending a call, or checking social media while reading an email—we were increasing our efficiency. However, recent sociological research suggests that so-called constant connectivity is actually a myth.

Actually, the human mind is not designed to process two different communication streams simultaneously. Instead, it "alternates" its attention back and forth between inputs. Each time we switch, there is a "reconnection cost"—a brief delay where the brain must refocus. While these delays only last a fraction of a second, they add up. Studies show that constant interruptions can reduce performance by as much as 40%.

Beyond efficiency, continuous connection also affects the depth of our relationships. When our attention is divided, we are more likely to make errors and less likely to remember what was said. This is particularly concerning in professional environments. Workers who check messages during meetings tend to have lower productivity than those who stay focused on the speaker.

Furthermore, constant information-switching can lead to increased stress and mental fatigue. The brain feels exhausted because it is constantly being forced to restart its processing. In the long run, this habit may even weaken our ability to engage in "deep thinking"—the capacity to focus without distraction on a cognitively demanding problem.

In a society that values speed, it takes courage to slow down. By practicing "mindful disconnection"—engaging in one conversation at a time with full attention—we can not only improve our performance but also rediscover a sense of calm in our busy lives.
