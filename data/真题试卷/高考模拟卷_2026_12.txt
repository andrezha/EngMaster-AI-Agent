When I was sixteen years old, my father gave me an old, traditional ceramic vase. While other teenagers were busy playing video games, I spent my afternoons sitting in the study room, trying to figure out how to capture the elegant patterns on the smooth surface. To me, pottery was a silent language that allowed me to convey emotions I could not express with simple words.

However, my dedication was not appreciated by everyone. My physics teacher, Mr. Hill, once listened to my explanations about art and commented, "Shaping clay is just moving material. Real innovation requires a lab and a scientific background." His remarks felt like a heavy weight in my mind. For a few weeks, I kept the pottery tools in their box, believing that my love for it was somehow inferior to scientific instruments.

One quiet evening, I noticed a fragile piece of broken porcelain near our garden, looking dull and worn. There was such a touching stillness in its curves that I instinctively picked up my brush and began to glaze the surface. When the light hit the ceramic, it affected me in a way I had not anticipated. It was not just an object; it was a story of hope, endurance, and quiet resilience.

I decided to show the restored vase to Mr. Hill. He took a close look and examined the piece without making a single sound. Finally, he looked at me and said, "I was mistaken. You did not just move material; you created something that others would have completely overlooked. That is the true heart of an artisan."

That experience taught me that we should never allow the opinions of others to define our value. Everyone has their own creative path, and as long as we remain faithful to our artistic expression, our work will eventually find its own voice.

For a long time, the concept of "rapid learning" was promoted as a crucial technique for success in the fast-paced modern era. We assumed that by scanning multiple documents at once—skimming through reports while taking a phone call, or flipping through a textbook while watching a video—we were boosting our retention. However, recent cognitive research indicates that so-called rapid learning is actually a misconception.

In reality, the human brain is not built to process two dense sources of information at the same time. Instead, it "alternates" its focus back and forth between different inputs. Every time we alternate, there is a "processing penalty" —a temporary lag where the brain has to adjust. Although these lags only take a fraction of a second, they accumulate. Studies demonstrate that dividing attention can reduce comprehension by as much as 40%.

Beyond comprehension, rapid switching also affects the depth of our understanding. When our focus is split, we are more likely to overlook details and less likely to retain the material. This is especially problematic in academic environments. Students who divide their attention between reading and listening to background audio tend to achieve lower scores than those who focus entirely on the text.

Moreover, constant information-switching can result in elevated anxiety and mental exhaustion. The mind feels drained because it is constantly forced to reset its focus. Over time, this practice might even weaken our ability to engage in "deep reflection"—the capacity to contemplate without distraction on a complex problem.

In a culture that prioritizes haste, it requires patience to slow down. By adopting "slow study"—reading one passage at a time with full concentration—we can not only enhance our comprehension but also regain a feeling of peace in our demanding lives.
